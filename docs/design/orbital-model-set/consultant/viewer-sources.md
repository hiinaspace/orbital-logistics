# Viewer and hobby references for the "model train set for spaceships" idea

Compiled 2026-10-07. Labels: [sourced] = read or returned by a search/fetch this session (URL + date of page where known; all pages accessed 2026-10-07); [inference] = my reading, not stated by a source. Paraphrased throughout.

## 1. NASA's Eyes (eyes.nasa.gov / science.nasa.gov/eyes)

- [sourced] Eyes on the Solar System is a browser 3D view you can fast-forward or rewind; NASA's Eyes landing page gives 1950-2050 and says it covers 150+ missions (Voyager, Cassini, OSIRIS-REx, New Horizons, Artemis, Europa Clipper preview). https://science.nasa.gov/eyes/ (accessed 2026-10-07). An older JPL news item says 126 spacecraft and a 1949-2049 span, and that you can rotate objects, compare them, and change perspective and lighting. https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/explore-the-solar-system-with-nasas-new-and-improved-3d-eyes/ (undated in fetch)
- [sourced] Positions and orientations are "based on real data from JPL" (Wikipedia summary). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA%27s_Eyes . A federal data catalog entry credits NASA's NAIF as data provider. https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/eyes-on-the-solar-system
- SPICE: [sourced, weak] a 2012 UnmannedSpaceflight forum post claims Eyes uses the same SPICE kernels (http://www.unmannedspaceflight.com/lofiversion/index.php/t6923.html). [sourced] I found no official NASA/JPL page that states Eyes reads SPICE kernels. [inference] Very likely true given NAIF involvement, but treat as unconfirmed. Related and confirmed: NAIF documents a SPICE-enabled version of the separate Cosmographia viewer (https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/naif/cosmographia_details.html, listed in search results only; not fetched).
- [sourced] Trajectory View (2026-04-30, launched with the Artemis II release): shows a mission's whole trajectory from a chosen camera position; "Draw Mission" frames the Earth-Moon system, "Draw Near Moon" puts the camera behind the Moon with Earth in view. Initial missions Artemis I/II, Voyager 1/2; Cassini added in the 2026-06-30 release as cumulative orbit lines (the "ball of yarn"). https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/eyes/2026/04/30/trajectory-view-feature-goes-live/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/eyes/2026/07/01/nasas-eyes-software-june-release-notes/
- [sourced] The 2026-06-30 release also added Share/embed options, including removing or minimizing the time controls and showing minor moons and the heliosphere. (same release-notes URL). So the time controls are a first-class, hideable UI element.
- [sourced] Sibling "Eyes" apps (landing page above): Eyes on Asteroids (30,000+ NEOs, next five close approaches, DART impact replay), Eyes on the Earth (Earth-observing satellites in real time; ride along with the ISS; ~20 years of data playback), Eyes on Exoplanets, DSN Now (live Deep Space Network links, refreshed about every 5 s), Mars Relay Network (past/present/future orbiter-lander comms, watch overflights from surface or orbit), Mars 2020 EDL, Experience Curiosity/InSight, Spacecraft AR.
- Camera: [sourced] The pages I could read confirm rotate/perspective and mission-specific camera positions (Trajectory View). [not found] I did not find official documentation of the "attach to spacecraft / look-from / look-at" camera modes or of the playback-rate presets. Do not claim them from this file; check the app directly.
- [sourced] Artemis tracking: AROW (Artemis Real-time Orbit Website), published 2026-03-06, last updated 2026-05-01. Shows Orion position relative to Earth and Moon, distance from each, mission elapsed time, path and milestones, Apollo landing sites; driven by Orion sensor data via Mission Control from about a minute after liftoff through reentry. NASA app adds an AR tracker. NASA also published state vectors and downloadable ephemeris for third-party visualizers. https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/track-nasas-artemis-ii-mission-in-real-time
- What it does not offer: [inference, from the feature lists above] No fleet or cargo logistics, no schedules or timetables, no user-authored maneuvers or counterfactual what-ifs; everything shown is a real mission's recorded or predicted trajectory. The closest thing to a "traffic" view is the Mars Relay Network and DSN Now, which visualize communication events between many assets, not transport.

## 2. Other things people watch (short)

- Kerbal Space Program Tracking Station / Map View: [sourced] lists every active mission with filters; map view shows orbits, patched conics and maneuver nodes (gated by building level in career mode); other vessels' positions visible. https://wiki.kerbalspaceprogram.com/wiki/Tracking_Station , https://wiki.kerbalspaceprogram.com/wiki/Map_View . [inference] The player's own vessels are the "fleet"; no scheduling or automation of recurring traffic in stock play (mods exist, not researched).
- Universe Sandbox: [sourced] N-body (Newtonian) physics sandbox where you build and perturb systems (stars, planets, moons, rings, black holes); first released 2008. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe_Sandbox . Watching = bodies evolving and colliding. [inference] No ships, cargo or schedules.
- SpaceEngine: [sourced] 1:1 procedural-plus-real-data planetarium of the observable universe; free movement through space and time. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceEngine . Watching = tourism. [inference] No traffic.
- Orbiter (open source since 2021, MIT): [sourced] Newtonian spaceflight simulator of the solar system; by its own description no predefined missions, aliens, or goods to trade. https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2021/08/orbiter-space-flight-simulator-goes-open-source . Watching = your own flights. [inference] Fleets only via add-ons; I did not check the 2024 edition.
- Celestia: [sourced] free/GPL space explorer spanning galaxy clusters to meter-scale craft, with a large community add-on ecosystem, including fictional universes. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestia . Watching = scripted/add-on scenes. [inference] Add-on scenes are the nearest precedent for "curated sets", but no logistics.
- Solar System Scope: not researched (no source); skip or treat as a pure educational viewer.
- Net: [inference] None of these has scheduled traffic, fleets or cargo as a watch-target; the "moving assets with purpose" niche appears unoccupied among these. Absence-of-evidence from a shallow search, not a market survey.

## 3. Model railroading: running vs operating

- [sourced] Community framing: the real split is often described as "continuous run" vs "point-to-point operations", not as "operating vs railfanning"; many layouts have a continuous loop for casual running but are operated point-to-point in sessions. This is hobbyist forum convention, not an official NMRA definition. Search summary of https://forum.trains.com/t/operations-vs-continuous-layouts/221253 (page returned 403 on fetch; I only have the search snippet, 2026-10-07).
- [sourced] An NMRA Potomac Division intro describes operations as a role-playing game in which operators use model trains to simulate real train movements and crew duties (OPSIG-hosted PDF; fetch returned binary, only the search snippet is available). https://opsig.org/files/resources/Abrams%20Intro%20Model%20RR%20Ops%2027Mar11.pdf
- [sourced] An NMRA Gateway Division article (2004-11-28, Bob Johnson) calls operation "one of the world's most magnificent board games": layout as board, trains as paths, rolling stock as tokens, waybills as card deck, and the chance that trains make their meets as the probability element. Success means the right cars arrive roughly on time. A "string diagram" schedule shows where trains meet and fits work into a fixed-length session. https://www.gatewaynmra.org/2004/essence-of-model-railroad-operations/
- [sourced] Car cards and waybills: each car carries a card with a pocket; a waybill gives a destination, typically four numbered steps with one visible; the owner turns waybills between sessions so cars advance to their next destination. Cards sit in hold/pick-up/set-out boxes, with a hold box simulating loading/unloading delay. Switch lists tell a crew where each car goes and are commonly computer-generated. Trains.com how-to series, e.g. https://www.trains.com/?p=81374 (parts 1-3; undated in results).
- [sourced] Switch list vs manifest: JMRI Operations (OperationsPro) generates a manifest per train (pickups/set-outs with each car's current and target locations) and switch lists per location from user-entered rosters, locations, routes and per-industry rules about car types and loads. It will merge empties with car requests and track cars. https://jmri.org/help/en/manual/JMRI_OPS_UsersGuide/Ops_Start.shtml ; https://jmri.org/help/en/manual/JMRI_OPS_UsersGuide/Ops_TrainsBuild.shtml
- [sourced] Fast clock: a clock running faster than real time so a session covers a longer railroad day. Common ratios 4:1 and 6:1; reported up to 12:1; no standard. A 4:1 clock turns a 24 h day into about 6 real hours. Caveat: it suits running time between towns but not yard work, which does not compress (15 real minutes of switching feels like an hour at 4:1). Another view: keep activity count per real time constant and the ratio matters less; some run 1:1 with a sequence timetable (trains meet at points, not clock times). https://dccwiki.com/Fast_Clock ; https://forum.trains.com/t/fast-clock-am-i-missing-something-here/231072/1 (forum, via search summaries).
- [sourced] TT&TO critique (forum): crews decide when to proceed from the timetable and a shared clock; hobbyists note short layouts and too many trains per hour undermine it. (search summary, trains.com forum threads)
- [sourced] Automation: JMRI Dispatcher runs trains manually or automatically: Transits (routes of Sections) + Trains = Active Trains, auto allocation/release of Sections with priority and meets, timed station stops in fast-clock minutes, hand off to manual mode for switching. Its docs state it is meant to make dispatching easier and more fun, not to be prototypical, and that multi-train auto-allocation is still under development and needs supervision. "Dispatcher System" adds auto-created stations and timetables. https://jmri.org/help/en/package/jmri/jmrit/dispatcher/Dispatcher.shtml ; https://decoderpro.jmri.org/help/en/html/apps/DispatcherPro/index.shtml
- [sourced] Published plans: Kalmbach/Model Railroader sells long-running plan collections ("101 Track Plans for Model Railroaders" since 1956; "101 More Track Plans" 2010; "102 Realistic Track Plans"; "Mid-Size Track Plans for Realistic Layouts", 2008). Caveat from the sellers: most published plans were never built and ship as track plans only; Kalmbach advises mining them for ideas rather than copying foot by foot; plans tied to project-layout articles come with more build detail. https://shop.trains.com/products/102-realistic-track-plans (and search results for the book series).
- [inference] Analogy to curated mission sets: a published plan is a pre-validated scenario you adopt, then customize. The caveat that most were never built is a warning that "curated" sets need to have been run end to end.

## 5. What makes watching durable vs a screensaver [all inference, grounded as noted]

1. Distinct identities with jobs. Waybills, manifests and "trains as paths" make each mover someone going somewhere for a reason (Gateway NMRA, trains.com). Anonymous loops are the "roundy-round" case the forum thread contrasts with operations.
2. Visible state that changes: cargo/fuel/loads on board, a waybill step that advances. In railroading the car card is the state; Eyes' DSN Now and Mars Relay show link state per asset. Without legible state, movement carries no information.
3. Meets and conflicts. The board-game framing hinges on trains making their meets; JMRI's Dispatcher auto-sets meets. Orbital analog (rendezvous, shared ports, phasing windows) should be shown, not hidden. Speculative on which orbital interactions read well.
4. A clock with structure. A fast clock plus timetable gives a session an arc (a "day"); rate control is standard in both hobby and Eyes. But the fast-clock caveat suggests non-compressible activities (dwell, docking) will feel wrong if the rate is scaled blindly; consider letting the viewer see the rate.
5. Multiple viewpoints. Eyes' trajectory camera and frame-style views, and AROW's AR mode, show the same event reads very differently by viewpoint; the existing reference-frame switching is the matching feature here. Moderate confidence.
6. Light intervention. Operations sessions and JMRI Dispatcher both pair automation with a hand-off to manual control, and Dispatcher's authors say supervision is expected. Suggests "automatic by default, nudge-able" rather than fully hands-off or fully manual.
7. A reason to come back: sessions with a start, an end and a schedule (string diagram), or changing state between sessions (waybills turned). A perpetual steady-state loop lacks that.
8. Curated starting points work for both audiences. Published plans and Celestia add-on scenes show people adopt ready-made setups; Orbiter's lack of predefined scenarios is a stated feature of that sim. Caution: unrun plans disappoint, so curate only what has been executed.
9. Shareable output. The Zachtronics GIF culture (one loop captured and shared) suggests a loop-capture/permalink feature for a scene; Eyes' deep-link plans and embeds point the same way. Weak evidence, anecdotal.
10. Screensaver signature: identical loops with no state change, no interaction between movers, one fixed viewpoint, and no time structure. Several of the viewers in section 2 sit here when nothing is being played. Speculation about user reaction; the sources do not measure it.
